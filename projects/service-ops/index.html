Where 1.9 million service requests get stuck

An operational reporting dashboard for a public contact centre: demand, channel mix, resolution against a 48-hour SLA, and the data-quality issues that quietly distort those numbers. Built on six months of real NYC 311 service requests.

The brief

A contact-centre operations lead wants one page that answers four questions every Monday: how much demand came in, are we meeting the 48-hour SLA, which channels and teams are falling behind, and when should we staff up. They also want to know how far they can trust the numbers.

Data and model

Loading…

The raw extract is loaded into SQLite and modelled as a star schema: one fact table with a row per request, and dimension tables for agency, channel, borough and complaint type. Long tails are rolled up (the 8 largest agencies and 25 largest complaint types keep their names, everything else becomes "Other") so every chart stays readable. Cleaning rules sit in one SQL file, so every number on this page uses the same definitions.

The browser receives small pre-aggregated cubes (week × agency × channel × borough, and similar), not raw rows, which keeps the page fast while every filter still recalculates every KPI.

Findings

1. The headline SLA hides the problem

Across everything, 69.6% of measurable closures met the 48-hour SLA. But NYPD handles 46.6% of all requests (mostly illegal parking and noise) and closes 99.9% of them inside 48 hours. Take NYPD out and the rest of the city resolves only 38.8% within 48 hours. A single blended number tells the operations lead things are fine when most teams are missing the target.

2. Housing is the biggest backlog risk

Housing (HPD) receives 325,995 requests, 17% of the total, and resolves just 17.6% within 48 hours. 52.8% take longer than 7 days, and the median is 181 hours (about 7.5 days). The slow work is concentrated: 64.5% of unsanitary-condition complaints and 54.2% of plumbing complaints take over 7 days. Heat and hot water, by contrast, almost always close within a week (0.4% over 7 days), which shows the team can move fast on a complaint type it prioritises.

3. "Faster channels" is mostly a mix effect

The mobile app looks like the best channel (92.3% within 48h, against 50.3% for phone). But 80% of app requests go to NYPD, the fastest agency. Compare like with like and the gap mostly disappears: for sanitation (DSNY), online, phone and app resolve within 48h at 61%, 63% and 56%. Pushing customers to the app would not, on its own, speed anything up.

4. Parks has an ageing backlog

Parks (DPR) still has 39.3% of its requests open at the end of the period. Even a quarter of the Parks requests from April (2,195 of 8,638) were still open at the end of September. Those are not in any resolution-time figure, because they have not closed yet, so open-request ageing needs its own report.

5. One week broke the pattern

Weekly volume is steady at roughly 69,000 to 81,000 requests, except the week of 29 June, which reached 87,511. Drilling in, the extra demand was mostly Water System complaints, about 8,000 more than the weeks either side, plus street noise around 4 July. A one-off like this is worth a short note to the water team before it shows up as a missed SLA.

6. Night demand is a police-noise pattern

Overall demand peaks between 09:00 and 13:00. But 28.5% of NYPD requests arrive between 22:00 and 04:00, compared with 8.9% for every other agency. Out-of-hours staffing should be planned around one agency's complaint types, not the city-wide curve.

Data quality: what I found and how I handled it

  • 28,503 requests were closed at the exact second they were created, mostly at Environmental Protection (13,226) and Transportation (9,651). These look like automatic or duplicate closures, not real fixes, so they are counted separately and kept out of every SLA figure. Left in, they would make both agencies look faster than they are.
  • 624 requests were closed before they were opened, almost all at Environmental Protection. They are flagged and excluded.
  • 120,004 requests were still open when the data was extracted. They are excluded from resolution times but reported as backlog, so slow teams cannot look better by simply not closing work.
  • The channel is unknown for 173,854 requests (8.8%), including every Buildings (DOB) request and 59% of Transportation's. Any channel strategy for those teams is currently guesswork.

Every rule is a flag in the fact table (dq_flag), so the excluded records can still be counted and audited rather than silently dropped.

Recommendations

  1. Report the SLA per agency, never only the blended figure. Show the city-wide number next to the figure without NYPD so the headline cannot hide underperformance.
  2. Give housing complaints SLAs by type. A single 48-hour target for HPD is missed four times out of five. Set realistic targets per complaint type, and run a weekly breach list for unsanitary conditions and plumbing, the two types that most often run past 7 days.
  3. Add an ageing report for open requests. Review anything open longer than 90 days with Parks first, and either close it with a reason or escalate it.
  4. Fix channel capture before investing in channel shift. Buildings and Transportation need channel recorded on every request, then compare channels within each agency, not across the city.
  5. Ask Environmental Protection about instant closures. If they are duplicates, merge them at intake; if they are auto-closures, report them as a separate outcome.

How it's built

  • Extract: six monthly CSV pulls from the NYC Open Data API (dataset erm2-nwe9).
  • Model: 02_model.sql builds the dimensions and fact table and flags data-quality issues.
  • KPIs: 03_kpi_views.sql defines resolution bands, the reporting cubes and exact medians.
  • Pipeline: build.py loads the CSVs, runs the SQL and exports the cubes to JSON.
  • Dashboard: plain JavaScript and SVG, no chart library, light and dark themes, keyboard-accessible filters.
